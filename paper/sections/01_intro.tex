\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that a consistent, effectively axiomatized theory that contains enough arithmetic cannot prove every arithmetic truth, and in particular cannot prove its own consistency~\cite{Godel1931,HilbertBernays1939}. Mathematics therefore grows by adding axioms. The theorems themselves say nothing about how to describe that growth: what stays fixed when a theory is extended, what changes, and how two different extensions should be compared. Foundational debates about new axioms often move between strength, maximality and mathematical practice without a common structural vocabulary~\cite{Maddy1988,Maddy1997}. This paper proposes one such vocabulary and proves what it can and cannot deliver.

\paragraph{Two ideas.}
The first idea is to treat a theory as a \emph{package}: a fixed closure rule, modelled as an idempotent map on a space of states. Deductive closure under a fixed set of axioms is the motivating example. It is idempotent, because closing a deductively closed set adds nothing. Iterating one package is then a different operation from switching to another package, and the two behave very differently. Iteration stops after one step. Switching is the only way to keep moving.

The second idea is to compare extensions only after the scoring rule has been fixed. A \emph{frozen ledger} assigns each candidate extension operator a yield and a cost, and efficiency means Pareto efficiency for that ledger. The ledger is also tied to a \emph{cone}, a set of inputs on which it looks at the operators. If the ledger cannot distinguish operators that agree on the cone, then efficiency belongs to whole agreement classes and not to individual syntactic presentations. One can then ask when an efficient operator can be traded for a designated canonical one, such as the consistency extension.

\paragraph{What is proved.}
The results fall into four groups. Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows how they depend on each other.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Packages and switching} (Section~\ref{sec:fixed-package}). A fixed idempotent package saturates after one application. Idempotence is equivalent to the absence of further growth at every input. If one package moves the output of another, the two maps differ at that output. Along any run of idempotent packages, the number of state changes is at most one plus the number of package switches (Theorem~\ref{thm:switch-budget}). Two explicit computable, monotone, inflationary closures on~$\N$ attain this bound at every horizon and produce unbounded growth (Proposition~\ref{prop:sharpness}).
\item \emph{Comparison on a frozen ledger} (Section~\ref{sec:frozen-frontier}). Efficiency transfers across operators that agree on the cone (Theorem~\ref{thm:efficiency-transfer}). A canonical family contained in the comparison domain supplies efficient representatives under every invariant ledger if and only if it meets every agreement class of the domain (Theorem~\ref{thm:ledger-boundary}). Over any class of ledgers that contains a constant ledger, efficiency alone therefore cannot produce canonical representatives.
\item \emph{The conditional main theorem} (Section~\ref{sec:conditional-lift}). For a ledger invariant under cone agreement and under an explicit contract with three clauses, a computable, monotone, efficient operator has an efficient, computable, monotone canonical representative that agrees with it on the cone (Theorem~\ref{thm:conditional-arithmetic-lift}). One clause requires the canonical family itself to be computable and monotone. A counterexample shows it cannot be dropped.
\item \emph{A concrete case in arithmetic} (Section~\ref{sec:arithmetic-instance}). In elementary arithmetic~$\EA$, a guarded consistency extension agrees with the ordinary consistency extension, up to provable equivalence, on a true cone. It strictly strengthens every consistent input in that cone, differs from the consistency extension outside it, and is efficient under a nonconstant invariant ledger under which the identity is dominated (Theorem~\ref{thm:guarded}). Walsh's dichotomy for the consistency operator~\cite{Walsh2020Note} gives an analogous result for a restricted class of operators (Theorem~\ref{thm:walsh-class}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What is formal and what is cited.}
Every internal result is checked in Lean~4 against mathlib~\cite{deMouraUllrich2021,Mathlib2020}. The formal statements use mathlib's \texttt{Computable} and \texttt{Monotone} predicates, not informal labels. The build audits every theorem and definition of the library for its kernel axioms. Only the three standard axioms of classical Lean appear, with no project axioms and no unfinished proofs (Appendix~\ref{app:lean-map}). The arithmetic results of Section~\ref{sec:arithmetic-instance} rest on two published theorems: the second incompleteness theorem for elementary presentations~\cite{ArtemovBeklemishev2005} and Walsh's dichotomy~\cite{Walsh2020Note}. Neither proof is reproduced in Lean. Each enters the formal statements as a named hypothesis, and the logical steps that use them are checked. Appendix~\ref{app:contract-imports} lists every assumption and import.

\paragraph{Six Birds vocabulary.}
The paper belongs to the Six Birds series, which describes emergent structure using six primitive operations: rewrite (P1), gating (P2), protocol or route mismatch (P3), staging and indexing (P4), packaging or closure (P5), and accounting or audit (P6)~\cite{Tsiokos2026Foundations,Tsiokos2026ToCount}. Here the primitives serve as interpretive labels only. A package is a P5 object. The ledger is a P6 object. The cone plays the role of the P4 index that fixes where comparison happens. Package changes are where P1 and P2 are read as acting. P3 plays no part in the proofs. The theorems never depend on these labels. In particular, the theorems show that two package maps differ but say nothing about \emph{which} mechanism changed them.

\paragraph{Related work.}
Closure operators and consequence relations go back to Tarski~\cite{TarskiConsequence1930,TarskiFixedPoint1955}. Extending theories by consistency and reflection statements is the subject of Turing's ordinal logics and Feferman's recursive progressions~\cite{Turing1939,Feferman1962}. It also underlies the modern analysis of reflection principles and reflection ranks~\cite{BeklemishevReflection,PakhomovWalsh2021}. The closest results to Section~\ref{sec:arithmetic-instance} are due to Montalbán and Walsh, and to Walsh. They compare recursive monotone operators with the consistency operator on true cones~\cite{MontalbanWalsh2019,Walsh2020Note}. Walsh also shows that the analogous classification by higher iterates of consistency fails: some recursive monotone operators, even with $\Pi_1$ values, oscillate cofinally between iterates~\cite{Walsh2022Evitable}. Our guarded case (Theorem~\ref{thm:guarded}) is proved directly from second incompleteness and propositional reasoning. Our restricted class (Theorem~\ref{thm:walsh-class}) is a corollary of Walsh's dichotomy. In both, our contribution is the frozen-ledger framing and the transfer of efficiency along these comparisons, not a new classification.
